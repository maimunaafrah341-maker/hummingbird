## Section 2 — Hazards Identification

Concentrated sulphuric acid is a colourless to dark brown oily liquid,
odourless, very dense, and severely corrosive to every tissue it
contacts. It causes deep chemical burns to skin and permanent damage to
eyes, and ingestion causes severe injury to the mouth, throat,
oesophagus and stomach.

Three separate mechanisms cause injury and they are frequently confused
with one another. The acid is corrosive, so it destroys tissue
chemically. It is strongly dehydrating, so it strips water out of
organic material, which is why contact with skin, wood or paper chars
them. And it releases a large amount of heat when it mixes with water,
so a dilution that is done in the wrong order produces a thermal injury
on top of the chemical one.

Mist or vapour from hot acid, or generated by splashing, is corrosive
to the respiratory tract and eyes and is a recognised carcinogen in
occupational settings. Exposure limit is taken here as 0.2 mg/m³ as an
eight-hour time-weighted average for the mist.

The liquid is roughly 1.8 times as dense as water. A spill spreads
further and carries more mass than an equivalent visible volume of
water would suggest, and it will find floor drains readily.

Sulphuric acid is not flammable, but it generates hydrogen — which is
flammable over an extremely wide range in air and needs almost no
ignition energy — when it contacts most common metals. A contained
acid spill in a plant room can therefore become an explosion hazard
without anything having burned.

## Section 4 — First-Aid Measures

Speed matters more than anything else that follows. The interval before
irrigation begins governs the depth of the eventual burn.

For skin contact, immediately flush the affected area with large
quantities of running water for at least 30 minutes, removing
contaminated clothing, footwear and watch straps while flushing.
Contaminated clothing holds acid against the skin and continues to
injure it after the visible splash has been washed away.

For eye contact, irrigate with clean water for at least 30 minutes
holding the eyelids apart, and continue irrigating during transport to
medical care.

Do not attempt to neutralise acid on skin or in the eye with sodium
bicarbonate, soap solution, lime or any other alkali. Neutralisation is
strongly exothermic, and performing it on tissue that is already burned
adds a thermal burn to a chemical one. Water in quantity, for a long
time, is the treatment.

Where dry or highly concentrated acid is present as a crust or residue,
brushing off the bulk material before irrigation is appropriate,
because the first small volume of water contacting concentrated acid
generates significant heat. Once irrigation starts, it must be with a
large and continuous flow — a trickle is worse than none, because it
supplies enough water to react and not enough to carry the heat away.

If acid has been swallowed, do not induce vomiting, which returns the
acid across the oesophagus a second time and risks aspiration. Do not
attempt neutralisation in the stomach. Do not give anything by mouth to
a casualty who is drowsy or unconscious. If the casualty is fully
conscious, small sips of water to dilute are acceptable. Obtain
immediate medical attention.

For inhalation of mist, move to fresh air and obtain medical attention;
delayed respiratory effects are possible.

## Section 5 — Fire-Fighting Measures

Sulphuric acid does not burn. The hazards in a fire are the hydrogen
generated by acid attacking metal, the corrosive mist driven off by
heat, and the violent reaction of the acid with any water applied to
it.

Use dry chemical, carbon dioxide or foam appropriate to the surrounding
materials to fight a fire near an acid spill. Do not direct a water jet
onto spilled concentrated acid. The heat of mixing flashes the water to
steam at the point of contact and ejects a spray of concentrated acid
outward, over a considerably wider area than the original spill and
generally in the direction of whoever is holding the hose.

Water spray may be used to cool intact containers exposed to fire and
to knock down acid mist, provided it is not played into the liquid
itself. Water used in this way becomes acidic and must be contained.

Fire-fighters must wear self-contained breathing apparatus and full
acid-resistant protective clothing. Structural fire kit is permeable to
acid and is not adequate for a spill response.

## Section 6 — Accidental Release Measures

Evacuate the immediate area, isolate it, and eliminate ignition sources
because of the hydrogen hazard described in Section 2. Ventilate
enclosed spaces if this can be done without entering them.

Responders must wear acid-resistant suit, gloves, boots and full face
protection, with respiratory protection where mist may be present.

Contain the spill with dry inert material — dry sand, earth or a
proprietary acid absorbent. Do not flush the spill to drain, and do not
use water to move or dilute a bulk spill.

Neutralisation of a contained spill with soda ash, lime or sodium
bicarbonate may be carried out by trained personnel, cautiously, from
the edge inward, adding the neutraliser slowly to the acid and never
the reverse. The reaction foams vigorously and releases carbon dioxide
and heat, and adding neutraliser quickly to a large volume produces a
boiling, spattering mass of acid. On a large spill, containment and
professional removal is preferable to neutralisation.

Collect neutralised residue for disposal as chemical waste. Wash the
area only after the bulk acid has been absorbed or neutralised, and
check the washings before allowing them to drain.

## Section 7 — Handling and Storage

Always add acid to water, slowly, with stirring. Never add water to
concentrated acid. Water added to acid floats on the denser acid and
the heat of mixing is released in a thin layer at the surface, which
can reach boiling point locally and eject acid violently out of the
vessel. Acid added to water disperses the same heat through the whole
volume. This ordering is the single most important handling rule for
this substance and it is the one most often reversed under time
pressure.

Store in a cool, dry, well-ventilated area with a bunded, acid-
resistant floor that does not drain to a public sewer. Keep containers
closed and upright.

Segregate from water-reactive materials, from strong bases and other
alkalis, from metals, from chlorates, perchlorates, permanganates,
carbides and fulminates, and from all organic and combustible
materials. Segregate by distance or barrier, not by labelling alone.

Use acid-resistant transfer equipment and never pressurise a carboy to
empty it. Provide an emergency shower and eyewash immediately adjacent
to any point where acid is decanted, and verify by test that they
deliver a large and sustained flow.

## Section 10 — Stability and Reactivity

Stable under the storage conditions described above. Hygroscopic;
absorbs water from the air.

Reacts violently and exothermically with water, particularly when water
is added to the acid rather than the reverse.

Attacks most common metals, including iron, steel, aluminium and zinc,
liberating hydrogen. Hydrogen is flammable from about 4 to 75 per cent
in air and ignites on very little energy, so an acid spill in contact
with structural steel or a metal bund in a poorly ventilated space
creates an explosion risk that is not obvious from the substance's own
non-flammability.

Reacts violently with strong bases, releasing large amounts of heat.

Reacts dangerously with chlorates, perchlorates, permanganates,
carbides, fulminates and many organic compounds, in several cases
igniting them. Contact with organic material chars and may ignite it.

Hazardous decomposition products include sulphur oxides and, on contact
with metals, hydrogen.
