## Section 2 — Hazards Identification

Sodium hydroxide, commonly called caustic soda or lye, is supplied as
white deliquescent pellets, flakes or beads, or as a colourless
solution of varying strength. It is odourless and severely corrosive to
skin, eyes and the respiratory and digestive tracts.

Alkali burns are more dangerous than acid burns of comparable strength
and are consistently underestimated at the scene, for two reasons that
matter operationally. First, alkali saponifies fat and dissolves
protein rather than coagulating it, so there is no barrier layer formed
and the injury continues to penetrate for as long as the substance
remains in contact. Second, the initial contact is often not
particularly painful — caustic solution feels soapy and slippery on the
skin, and that slipperiness is the surface layer of the worker's own
skin being dissolved. A casualty may not report a splash until
significant damage has been done.

Eye contact is a sight-threatening emergency. Alkali penetrates the
cornea within seconds and can reach the anterior chamber, and the
outcome depends heavily on how quickly and how long irrigation is
carried out.

Dissolving solid caustic soda in water releases a large amount of heat.
A concentrated solution can approach boiling in an open vessel, and hot
caustic solution splashed onto skin delivers a thermal and a chemical
burn together.

Dust from pellets or flakes is corrosive to the airway. Occupational
exposure limit is taken here as 2 mg/m³ as a ceiling value, not a
time-weighted average — the limit is not to be exceeded at any moment.

## Section 4 — First-Aid Measures

For eye contact, irrigate immediately with clean water and continue for
at least 60 minutes, holding the eyelids apart, and continue during
transport to medical care. The irrigation time for alkali eye injury is
substantially longer than for acid, and stopping early because the
casualty reports relief is a recognised cause of avoidable sight loss.
Obtain ophthalmic assessment for any caustic eye exposure, however
minor it appears.

For skin contact, flush with large quantities of running water for at
least 30 minutes while removing contaminated clothing, footwear and
jewellery. Continue flushing until the slippery, soapy feel of the skin
has gone; while it persists, alkali is still present and still
reacting.

Where solid caustic pellets or flakes are on the skin, brush off the
dry material first, then irrigate with a large and continuous flow.
Wetting solid caustic with a small quantity of water generates heat and
produces a concentrated hot solution in contact with the skin, which is
worse than either the dry solid or a proper flood of water.

Do not attempt to neutralise caustic on skin or in the eye with vinegar,
citric acid, boric acid or any other acidic solution. The reaction is
exothermic and takes place on tissue that is already injured. Water, in
quantity, for a long time, is the treatment.

If swallowed, do not induce vomiting. Returning caustic across the
oesophagus a second time compounds the injury and risks aspiration into
the lungs. Do not attempt neutralisation in the stomach. Do not give
anything by mouth to a casualty who is drowsy, convulsing or
unconscious. If the casualty is fully conscious and able to swallow,
small sips of water or milk to dilute are acceptable. Obtain immediate
medical attention.

Casualties with airway exposure to dust or mist require observation for
delayed swelling.

## Section 5 — Fire-Fighting Measures

Sodium hydroxide does not burn and will not support combustion. The
hazards in a fire are the heat generated when water contacts the solid,
the corrosive nature of the resulting solution, and the hydrogen
produced by caustic attacking certain metals.

Use an extinguishing medium appropriate to the surrounding fire. Where
solid caustic soda is stored, prefer dry chemical or carbon dioxide in
the immediate vicinity.

Do not apply a water jet directly onto bulk solid caustic soda. The
heat of solution is substantial, and hosing a drum of pellets produces
a hot, concentrated, strongly corrosive liquid that spreads across the
floor and spatters. Where water must be used on a surrounding fire,
apply it so that run-off does not reach stored solid caustic.

Caustic soda in contact with aluminium, zinc, tin and their alloys
liberates hydrogen, which is flammable and easily ignited. A caustic
spill onto aluminium plant or an aluminium bund inside a burning
building adds an explosion hazard.

Fire-fighters require self-contained breathing apparatus and full
chemical protective clothing. Run-off water will be strongly alkaline
and must be contained.

## Section 6 — Accidental Release Measures

Evacuate the immediate area and isolate it. Responders require
alkali-resistant suit, gloves, boots and full face protection, with
respiratory protection where dust or mist is present.

For a solid spill, sweep or shovel the dry material into a labelled
container for disposal, avoiding dust generation. Do not wash a bulk
solid spill down with water as the first action; dissolving it releases
heat and converts a contained, sweepable solid into a spreading
corrosive liquid that will run into drains and low areas. Recover the
bulk dry, then wash the residue.

For a solution spill, contain with dry inert absorbent — sand, earth or
a proprietary absorbent — and prevent entry to drains, sewers and
watercourses.

Neutralisation of a contained spill with a dilute weak acid may be
carried out by trained personnel, cautiously and from the edge inward,
adding the acid slowly to the caustic. The reaction is exothermic and
adding acid quickly produces violent boiling and spattering of caustic
solution. Do not attempt to neutralise a large spill; contain it and
arrange professional removal.

Wash the area down only after the bulk material has been recovered, and
check the pH of the washings before allowing them to drain.

## Section 7 — Handling and Storage

Always add caustic soda to water, slowly, with stirring, and never add
water to solid caustic soda. Water poured onto pellets generates intense
localised heat, can boil, and ejects concentrated hot caustic out of the
vessel. This ordering is the most important handling rule for this
substance, and it is the mirror image of the rule for sulphuric acid —
in both cases the concentrated material goes into the water, never the
reverse.

Keep containers tightly closed. Solid caustic soda is deliquescent: it
absorbs moisture and carbon dioxide from the air, cakes, and can
generate heat inside a drum left open in humid conditions.

Store in a cool, dry, well-ventilated area with an alkali-resistant
bunded floor that does not drain to a public sewer. Keep clear of any
area subject to flooding or roof leaks, since water reaching stored
solid caustic is itself the incident.

Segregate from acids, from water-reactive materials, and from
aluminium, zinc, tin and their alloys. Do not use aluminium containers,
funnels, scoops or fittings for caustic soda in any circumstances.

Provide an emergency shower and eyewash immediately adjacent to any
point where caustic is handled or decanted, and verify by test that
they deliver a large and sustained flow of clean water.

## Section 10 — Stability and Reactivity

Stable under the storage conditions described above. Deliquescent, and
absorbs carbon dioxide from the air to form sodium carbonate.

Dissolution in water is strongly exothermic.

Reacts violently and exothermically with acids. Neutralisation of bulk
material without control of the addition rate produces boiling and
spattering.

Attacks aluminium, zinc, tin and their alloys, liberating hydrogen,
which is flammable over a very wide range in air. This reaction is the
most commonly overlooked hazard of the substance, because caustic soda
is not itself flammable and the hydrogen is generated quietly at
ordinary temperatures.

Reacts with some organic halogen compounds and with nitro compounds,
in some cases violently. Attacks glass slowly, and attacks leather,
wool and other protein materials, including the protein of skin.

Hazardous decomposition products include hydrogen on contact with
susceptible metals.
