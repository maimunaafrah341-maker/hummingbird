# SAMPLE REGULATORY EXCERPTS — OSHA GENERAL INDUSTRY (29 CFR 1910)

**ILLUSTRATIVE SAMPLE. THIS IS NOT REGULATORY TEXT AND IS NOT QUOTED
FROM THE CODE OF FEDERAL REGULATIONS.**
Every section below is a hand-written paraphrase drafted for
development and testing of HazardWatch OS. Section numbers are included
because they make the samples realistic to work with, not because the
wording beneath them reproduces what those sections actually say. The
paraphrases are approximate, incomplete, and in places simplified to
the point of being wrong as law. Nothing here establishes any legal
duty and nothing here should be cited, quoted or relied upon. A
deployment used for real compliance must replace this file with the
actual regulatory text.

## 1910.151(c) — Emergency eyewash and drench facilities

Where the eyes or body of any person may be exposed to injurious
corrosive materials during their work, suitable facilities for quick
drenching or flushing of the eyes and body must be provided within the
work area for immediate emergency use.

The accepted interpretation of "within the work area" is that the
facility is reachable in about ten seconds of unimpeded travel, which
is commonly implemented as a distance of roughly fifteen metres on the
same level, along a path with no doors to open, no stairs to climb and
no obstruction to step around. The reason for the time limit rather
than a pure distance limit is that a casualty with a corrosive
substance in their eyes cannot see where they are going, so a route
that is short on a drawing may be impassable in practice.

Facilities must deliver clean water at a rate and for a duration
sufficient to support the irrigation times specified for the substances
in use, which for strong alkalis may be an hour or more. A unit that
delivers a brief trickle does not satisfy this requirement, and a
trickle applied to concentrated corrosives can be actively harmful.

Plumbed units should be activated weekly to flush the line and to
confirm they still function; self-contained units require their fluid
to be maintained and replaced. An eyewash that has never been tested
is, in practice, unlikely to work on the day it is needed.

## 1910.1200 — Hazard communication

Employers must maintain a safety data sheet for every hazardous
chemical in the workplace, and those sheets must be readily accessible
to employees in their work area during each work shift.

"Readily accessible" means the worker can get to the information
without asking permission, without a password they do not have, and
without waiting for a supervisor to unlock an office. A binder locked
in a manager's room during the night shift does not satisfy this
requirement, and neither does an electronic system that is unavailable
when the site network is down. Where sheets are held electronically, a
reliable backup must exist for the situation in which the electronic
system cannot be reached — which includes the emergency the sheets are
needed for.

Containers must be labelled with the product identifier and the
appropriate hazard warnings, and labels must not be removed or
defaced. Decanted material in an unlabelled secondary container is a
recurring cause of a responder treating the wrong substance.

Employees must be trained on the hazards of the chemicals in their work
area, on the measures they can take to protect themselves, and on the
details of the employer's hazard communication programme, at the time
of their initial assignment and whenever a new hazard is introduced.

## 1910.134 — Respiratory protection in IDLH atmospheres

An atmosphere that is immediately dangerous to life or health requires
a full-facepiece pressure-demand self-contained breathing apparatus, or
a combination supplied-air respirator with an auxiliary self-contained
supply. Air-purifying respirators — cartridge and canister masks — are
not adequate in an IDLH atmosphere and must not be used in one. A
cartridge mask offers no protection where the atmosphere is
oxygen-deficient, and its cartridge has a finite and unmonitored
capacity for the contaminant.

Where employees enter an IDLH atmosphere, at least one trained employee
must be located outside it, in visual, voice or signal-line
communication with those inside, equipped for and trained in rescue,
and provided with the equipment needed to effect it. The employer must
be notified before the standby person enters to attempt a rescue.

Respirators must be selected for the specific contaminant, fit-tested
to the individual wearer, inspected before each use and maintained.
Facial hair that interferes with the sealing surface prevents an
adequate seal, and a respirator that does not seal provides confidence
without protection.

## 1910.146 — Permit-required confined spaces

A confined space that contains or may contain a hazardous atmosphere is
a permit-required confined space, and entry to it requires a written
permit, atmospheric testing before and during entry, an attendant
stationed outside for the duration, and rescue provisions arranged
before entry rather than improvised afterwards.

Atmospheric testing must be carried out in the order oxygen first, then
flammable gases and vapours, then toxic contaminants, and must sample
the space at the top, middle and bottom. Testing only at the opening
misses exactly the stratified accumulation that makes these spaces
lethal — a heavier-than-air contaminant sits at the bottom, which is
where the worker's head will be when they reach the floor.

The attendant must remain outside the space, must monitor the
authorised entrants, and must not enter the space to attempt a rescue.
A very large proportion of confined space fatalities are would-be
rescuers who entered without equipment to help a colleague who had
collapsed, and who then collapsed for the same reason. Rescue must be
performed from outside the space wherever possible, using retrieval
equipment, and otherwise only by trained rescuers with breathing
apparatus.

## 1910.120(q) — Emergency response to hazardous substance releases

An employer whose employees may respond to a release of a hazardous
substance must have a written emergency response plan in place before
the response, covering roles, lines of authority, communication,
evacuation routes, decontamination, and the criteria for calling in
outside assistance.

Responders must be trained to a level appropriate to the role they will
actually perform. First responders at the awareness level are trained
to recognise a release, initiate the emergency response sequence and
protect themselves — and specifically to take no further action.
Employees who will attempt to stop a release from a safe distance, or
who will approach it, require substantially more training and are
required to work under an incident command system.

No employee who has not been trained to the required level may attempt
to stop a release, plug a leak, or enter the area of a release. The
untrained employee's correct action is to raise the alarm, evacuate,
and account for people — and the plan must make this explicit, because
in the absence of an explicit instruction the instinct of a competent
worker who knows the plant is to go and deal with it.

An incident commander must be identified and must be the person who
authorises entry, sets the isolation distances and authorises re-entry
once the release is controlled.
