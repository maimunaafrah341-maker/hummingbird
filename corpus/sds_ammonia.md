## Section 2 — Hazards Identification

Anhydrous ammonia is a colourless gas with an extremely sharp,
suffocating odour, handled industrially as a liquefied gas under
pressure and used widely as a refrigerant. It is corrosive and acutely
toxic by inhalation, and it is flammable within a narrow range.

The gas has a strong affinity for water and therefore attacks moist
tissue preferentially: eyes, mouth, throat, upper airway and any sweaty
or damp skin. Injury is alkaline in character, which matters
clinically, because alkali burns keep penetrating deeper into tissue
long after the initial contact rather than forming a limiting layer of
coagulated protein the way acid burns tend to.

Liquid ammonia released from a pressurised system flashes to gas and
absorbs a great deal of heat doing so, so direct contact with the
liquid or with the cold vapour stream produces frostbite in addition to
chemical burns. Anyone handling a liquid release is facing two
different injury mechanisms on the same square centimetre of skin.

Pure ammonia gas is lighter than air and will rise and disperse. This
is widely and dangerously over-generalised. A large release of
refrigerated liquid produces a cold, dense aerosol of gas and suspended
droplets that behaves as a heavy cloud at first, hugging the ground and
flowing into low areas exactly as chlorine would, until it warms enough
to lift. Do not treat low ground as safe during a large or refrigerated
release.

The odour is unmistakable and detectable far below harmful
concentrations, but as with chlorine it must never be used to declare
an area clear. Exposure limit is taken here as 25 ppm as an eight-hour
time-weighted average, with an IDLH value of 300 ppm. Ammonia is
flammable in air between roughly 15 and 28 per cent by volume, a range
that is hard to reach outdoors but readily reached inside a plant room.

## Section 4 — First-Aid Measures

Move the affected person to fresh air, upwind of the release, and do
not attempt entry into a plant room or confined space without breathing
apparatus and a standby person.

For eye contact, irrigate immediately with clean water for at least
20 minutes, holding the eyelids open, and continue irrigation during
transport to medical care. Alkali eye injury is a genuine emergency and
the duration of irrigation is the single factor most strongly
associated with the outcome. Do not stop early because the casualty
says the pain has eased.

For skin contact, remove contaminated clothing while flushing with
copious water and continue for at least 20 minutes. Where clothing has
frozen to the skin, thaw it in place with lukewarm water rather than
pulling it away, which removes skin with it.

Do not attempt to neutralise ammonia on skin or in the eye with any
acidic solution, including vinegar, dilute acetic acid, citrus juice or
any proprietary neutraliser. Neutralisation is an exothermic reaction
occurring on already-damaged tissue and adds a thermal burn to a
chemical one. Water, in quantity, for a long time, is the treatment.

If ammonia has been swallowed, do not induce vomiting: the caustic
liquid injures the oesophagus a second time on the way back up, and
vomit may be aspirated. Do not attempt to neutralise it in the stomach.
If the casualty is fully conscious and able to swallow, small sips of
water are acceptable. Give nothing by mouth to a casualty who is
drowsy, convulsing or unconscious.

Casualties with significant inhalation exposure require medical
observation for delayed airway swelling and pulmonary oedema even if
they appear to recover at the scene.

## Section 5 — Fire-Fighting Measures

Ammonia is flammable between approximately 15 and 28 per cent in air.
Outdoors it usually disperses below that range; inside a compressor
house, cold store or other enclosed plant space it can readily form an
explosive atmosphere, and that space may also contain the ignition
source.

Stop the flow of gas if the isolation valve can be reached safely. Do
not extinguish a burning ammonia release while gas continues to flow —
extinguishing the flame converts a visible, localised, largely
consumed release into an invisible toxic cloud accumulating in the same
building. Control the fire, cool the surroundings, and isolate the
source.

Use water spray to knock down and disperse vapour and to cool exposed
containers. Do not direct a water jet into a pool of liquid ammonia.
Water is much warmer than the boiling liquid and adds heat to it,
producing vigorous boiling and a far larger vapour cloud than the pool
was generating on its own. Apply water as a fine spray to the vapour
above the pool, not into the pool itself.

Water used for vapour suppression becomes strongly alkaline ammonia
solution and must be contained and prevented from entering drains or
watercourses, where it is severely toxic to fish.

Fire-fighters require self-contained breathing apparatus and full
chemical protective clothing.

## Section 6 — Accidental Release Measures

Raise the alarm, evacuate crosswind then upwind, and account for
personnel by name. Ventilate enclosed plant rooms remotely if fixed
ventilation can be started from outside; do not enter to open a door or
start a fan.

Isolate the area for at least 100 metres for a small leak and
considerably further downwind for a large one. During a large or
refrigerated release, evacuate low-lying ground as well, for the reason
given in Section 2 — the initial cold cloud is dense and does not
behave like the buoyant gas that ammonia becomes later.

Only trained responders in self-contained breathing apparatus and
protective clothing may approach. Isolate at source where the valve is
reachable without entering the cloud.

Ammonia must never be mixed with chlorine, with hypochlorite bleach or
with any other chlorine donor as part of a response. The reaction
produces chloramines and potentially nitrogen trichloride, adding an
explosive and a second toxic gas to an incident that already has one.
On a site holding both substances this is the most consequential
mistake available to a responder, and it is usually made with good
intentions by someone trying to neutralise a spill.

Do not allow ammonia or ammonia-contaminated water to enter drains,
sewers or watercourses.

## Section 7 — Handling and Storage

Store in a cool, dry, well-ventilated area away from heat and direct
sunlight, with cylinders upright and secured. Refrigeration plant must
have gas detection in the plant room, ventilation that can be started
from outside it, and an emergency stop that isolates the compressors
from a safe position.

Segregate ammonia from chlorine, from hypochlorites, from strong acids
and from halogens by distance or by a fire-resistant barrier. Storing
ammonia and chlorine in the same room is a recognised precursor to a
multi-substance incident and must not be done regardless of how the
containers are labelled.

Use steel or approved alloys for ammonia service. Copper, brass, bronze
and zinc and their alloys are attacked rapidly by ammonia and must
never be used for fittings, gauges, valves or lines. A brass fitting
installed as a convenient substitute is a common root cause of
subsequent releases.

Keep an eyewash station and emergency shower immediately adjacent to
any area where ammonia is transferred or decanted, and verify by test
that they run clean water at an adequate rate.

## Section 10 — Stability and Reactivity

Stable under the storage conditions described above.

Reacts violently with chlorine and with hypochlorites to form
chloramines and, under some conditions, the shock-sensitive explosive
nitrogen trichloride.

Reacts vigorously and exothermically with strong acids. Neutralisation
of a bulk ammonia spill with acid is not an approved response and has
caused injury through violent boiling and spattering.

Attacks copper, brass, bronze, zinc and their alloys, and forms
explosive compounds with mercury, silver and gold salts. Mercury
manometers must not be used on ammonia systems.

Hazardous decomposition products include nitrogen oxides and, in
contact with chlorine donors, chloramines and nitrogen trichloride.
