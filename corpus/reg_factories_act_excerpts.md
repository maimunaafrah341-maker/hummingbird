# SAMPLE REGULATORY EXCERPTS — FACTORIES ACT 1948 (INDIA)

**ILLUSTRATIVE SAMPLE. THIS IS NOT STATUTORY TEXT AND IS NOT QUOTED
FROM THE FACTORIES ACT 1948.**
Every section below is a hand-written paraphrase drafted for
development and testing of HazardWatch OS. Section numbers are included
because they make the samples realistic to work with, not because the
wording beneath them reproduces what those sections actually say. The
paraphrases are approximate, incomplete, and in places simplified to
the point of being wrong as law. State factory rules vary and impose
additional duties not reflected here. Nothing in this file establishes
any legal duty and nothing here should be cited, quoted or relied upon.
A deployment used for real compliance must replace this file with the
actual statutory text and the applicable state rules.

## Section 41B — Compulsory disclosure of information by the occupier

The occupier of a factory involving a hazardous process must disclose
all information concerning the dangers of that process, including
health hazards and the measures to overcome them, to the workers
employed in the factory, to the Chief Inspector, to the local
authority, and to the general public in the vicinity.

The duty runs to the workers directly, not only to the regulator. A
hazard assessment that exists in a file submitted to an authority, and
that the people working alongside the substance have never been shown,
does not discharge this obligation.

At the time of registering a factory involving a hazardous process, the
occupier must lay down a detailed policy with respect to the health and
safety of the workers, and must communicate it to the Chief Inspector
and the local authority.

The occupier must draw up an on-site emergency plan and detailed
disaster control measures, and must make known to the workers employed
and to the general public living in the vicinity the safety measures
required to be taken in the event of an accident. A plan that has not
been communicated to the people who would have to act on it is not an
emergency plan; it is a document about one.

Workers must be informed of the imminent danger to their health and
safety arising from any accident or from any process, and of the
remedial action to be taken.

## Section 41C — Specific responsibility in relation to hazardous processes

The occupier of every factory involving a hazardous process must
maintain accurate and up-to-date health records, or medical records, of
the workers who are exposed to any chemical, toxic or other harmful
substance which is manufactured, stored, handled or transported in the
factory. Those records must be accessible to the workers subject to
prescribed conditions.

The occupier must appoint persons who possess the qualifications and
experience prescribed for handling the hazardous substances involved,
and who are competent to supervise that handling. Supervision of a
hazardous process by a person qualified only in general production
management does not satisfy this requirement.

The occupier must provide for medical examination of every worker
before their employment in a hazardous process, and thereafter at
intervals not exceeding those prescribed, for as long as the worker
continues to be employed in that process. Where a worker is found to
have contracted an occupational disease, they must not be employed in
that process until they have been certified fit.

## Section 41H — Right of workers to warn about imminent danger

Where workers employed in a factory carrying on a hazardous process
have reasonable apprehension that there is a likelihood of imminent
danger to their lives or health, they may bring the matter to the
notice of the occupier, the agent, the manager or any other person in
charge of the factory, directly or through their representatives.

The person so notified must take immediate remedial action if satisfied
that there is a likelihood of imminent danger, and must send a report
of the action taken to the nearest Inspector.

If the person notified is not satisfied that there is such a
likelihood, they must refer the matter to the nearest Inspector
immediately, and the Inspector's decision on the question is final.

The practical significance of this section is that it places the duty
to act on the recipient of the warning, on a timescale set by the
danger rather than by the convenience of production. A worker who
reports a smell of gas, a leaking valve or an unlabelled drum has
discharged their part; the obligation then sits with the person in
charge, and deferring the question to a later shift or a scheduled
meeting is not compliance.

## Section 87 — Dangerous operations

Where the State Government is satisfied that any operation carried on
in a factory exposes the persons employed in it to a serious risk of
bodily injury, poisoning or disease, it may make rules applicable to
that operation.

Such rules may prohibit or restrict the employment of women,
adolescents or children in that operation; require periodical medical
examination of the persons employed and prohibit the employment of any
person not certified fit; require the provision of protective
equipment, of washing facilities and of accommodation for clothing kept
off during the operation; and require the exhaust of fumes, dust and
other impurities at their point of origin rather than their dilution in
the general workroom air.

The preference for control at source over general ventilation is
deliberate. Diluting a contaminant across a workroom reduces the
measured concentration without removing the exposure, and it moves the
hazard to workers who have no involvement in the operation that
produced it.

## Section 38 — Precautions in case of fire

Every factory must be provided with such means of escape in case of
fire as may be prescribed, and the means of escape must be maintained
free of obstruction and available for use at all material times.

Every factory must be provided with effective and clearly audible means
of giving warning in the case of fire to every person employed in it. A
warning system that is inaudible over the noise of the plant it
protects does not satisfy this requirement.

Every door affording a means of escape must not be locked or fastened
so that it cannot be easily and immediately opened from the inside
while any person is within the factory, and every such door must, save
in prescribed cases, be constructed to open outwards. Doors locked
against theft during a shift, and doors obstructed by stored material,
are the two most common ways in which a compliant escape route becomes
a non-compliant one without anything being formally changed.

Effective measures must be taken to ensure that all the workers are
familiar with the means of escape and have been adequately trained in
the routine to be followed in the case of fire.
