# SAMPLE SAFETY DATA SHEET — CHLORINE (Cl2)

## Section 2 — Hazards Identification

Chlorine is a greenish-yellow gas with a sharp, penetrating odour,
shipped and stored as a liquefied gas under pressure. It is acutely
toxic by inhalation and corrosive to the eyes and the entire
respiratory tract. Exposure at low concentrations causes stinging eyes,
throat irritation and coughing; at higher concentrations it causes
chest tightness, chemical bronchitis and pulmonary oedema, which may be
fatal.

The gas is roughly two and a half times heavier than air. A release
does not disperse upward; it flows downhill and collects in pits,
trenches, sumps, basements, inspection chambers and any other low-lying
space. Personnel standing at ground level in a depression can be in a
lethal concentration while the air two metres above them is breathable.
This single property governs almost every response instruction in this
document.

The odour is detectable well below the concentration that causes
injury, which is genuinely protective, but olfactory fatigue sets in
quickly. A worker who reports that the smell has faded has very
probably not moved into cleaner air; they have lost the ability to
smell the contaminant they are still standing in. Smell must never be
used to decide that an area has become safe.

Chlorine is not itself flammable, but it is a strong oxidiser and will
vigorously support the combustion of materials that do not normally
burn readily in air. Occupational exposure limit is taken here as
0.5 ppm as an eight-hour time-weighted average, with an IDLH value of
10 ppm.

## Section 4 — First-Aid Measures

Move the affected person to fresh air immediately, travelling
crosswind and then upwind of the release. Because the gas pools at low
level, moving the casualty upward and outward matters as much as moving
them away — carrying someone along a trench to a distant exit can keep
them in the cloud for the whole journey.

Do not enter a suspected chlorine atmosphere to perform a rescue
without self-contained breathing apparatus and a second trained person
standing by. Untrained rescuers entering low-lying spaces are a
recurring cause of multiple casualties from a single-casualty
incident.

If breathing has stopped, a trained responder should give assisted
ventilation using a bag-valve mask with supplemental oxygen. Direct
mouth-to-mouth ventilation is contraindicated: the rescuer inhales
contaminated air from the casualty's airway.

For eye contact, flush with clean water for at least 15 minutes,
holding the eyelids apart. For skin contact, remove contaminated
clothing while flushing and continue flushing the skin for at least
15 minutes. Contact lenses should be removed if this can be done
without delaying irrigation.

Every person with a significant inhalation exposure must be kept under
medical observation even if they appear to have recovered completely.
Pulmonary oedema can be delayed by up to 24 to 48 hours, and a casualty
who feels well at the scene may deteriorate severely overnight. Do not
discharge an exposed worker back to the floor on the strength of them
saying they feel fine.

## Section 5 — Fire-Fighting Measures

Chlorine does not burn. In a fire involving chlorine containers, the
hazard is the container itself: heat raises the internal pressure and
can rupture the cylinder or actuate its fusible plug, converting a
contained inventory into a sudden large release.

Cool containers exposed to fire with water spray applied from a
protected position or an unmanned monitor, from as far away as the
equipment allows.

Do not apply water directly to a chlorine leak. Water and chlorine form
hydrochloric and hypochlorous acids, which aggressively attack the
steel of the cylinder, valve and fittings. A water jet aimed at a small
leak with the intention of knocking down the gas reliably enlarges the
leak it was aimed at, and can turn a weeping valve into an open one.
Water spray may be used at a distance to suppress and direct a vapour
cloud, but never played onto the leaking vessel or its valve.

Never immerse a leaking cylinder in water, and never place it in a tank
or drum of water or caustic solution. The internal pressure of the
cylinder is higher than the head of liquid, so the leak does not stop;
water is instead drawn into the cylinder as it empties and cools,
producing violent internal corrosion.

Fire-fighters must wear self-contained breathing apparatus and full
protective clothing. Ordinary structural fire kit does not protect
against chlorine.

## Section 6 — Accidental Release Measures

Raise the alarm and evacuate immediately, moving crosswind first and
then upwind. Account for personnel by name; a headcount alone will not
identify who is still in a pit.

Establish an initial isolation zone of at least 100 metres in all
directions for a small leak, extended substantially downwind for a
large one, and deny entry to everyone without breathing apparatus.
Evacuate all low-lying areas within and beyond that radius, including
basements, cable trenches, drainage chambers and any adjacent building
with below-grade space, because the gas will find them.

Only personnel trained in chlorine emergency response, wearing
self-contained breathing apparatus and chemically resistant protective
clothing, may approach to attempt isolation. Shut off the supply at
source if the valve can be reached safely; do not attempt a repair on a
pressurised system.

If a cylinder is leaking, position it so that the leak is in the vapour
space rather than the liquid space — rotating a horizontal cylinder so
the leak points upward reduces the discharge rate by a large factor,
because liquid chlorine expands to several hundred times its volume as
gas.

Prevent the release from entering drains, sewers and watercourses.
Ventilate the area only after the source has been isolated, and
re-entry must be authorised on the basis of instrument readings, never
on the basis of smell.

## Section 7 — Handling and Storage

Store cylinders upright and secured against falling, in a cool, dry,
well-ventilated area that is not below ground level and does not drain
into an occupied space. Keep the storage temperature below 45 °C and
protect containers from direct sunlight and from any radiant heat
source.

Valve protection caps must be in place whenever a cylinder is not
connected and in use. Move cylinders with a proper trolley; never lift
one by its valve or cap, and never roll or drag a cylinder to move it
across a site.

Segregate chlorine from incompatible materials by distance or by a
fire-resistant barrier, not merely by labelling. Ammonia in particular
must never be stored in the same room, and the two must never be
handled in a way that allows an accidental connection between them.
Also segregate from hydrogen, hydrocarbons, turpentine, ether, finely
divided metals and all flammable materials.

Use only equipment, gaskets and lubricants specified for chlorine
service. Ordinary oils and greases react with chlorine and have caused
ignition inside valves. Systems must be scrupulously dry, because dry
chlorine is relatively compatible with steel while wet chlorine is
severely corrosive to it.

Fixed gas detection should be installed at low level, where the gas
will actually be, rather than at head height where a detector will
report clean air during a serious release.

## Section 10 — Stability and Reactivity

Chlorine is stable under the storage conditions described above. It is
a powerful oxidiser and reacts with a very wide range of materials.

Reaction with ammonia is the single most important incompatibility on a
site that holds both. Chlorine and ammonia react to form chloramines
and, under some conditions, nitrogen trichloride, which is a
shock-sensitive explosive. The reaction also generates a toxic vapour
that injures responders who arrived to deal with what they believed was
a single-substance leak. Never attempt to neutralise a chlorine release
with ammonia, or an ammonia release with chlorine or with any
hypochlorite bleach.

Mixtures of chlorine and hydrogen are explosive over a wide composition
range and can be initiated by ordinary daylight.

Chlorine reacts violently with finely divided metals, with many
organic compounds, and with turpentine and similar solvents, in several
cases igniting them without any external ignition source. Wet chlorine
corrodes most common metals. Contact with water forms hydrochloric and
hypochlorous acids.

Hazardous decomposition and reaction products include hydrogen
chloride, chloramines and nitrogen trichloride.
